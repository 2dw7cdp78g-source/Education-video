## File: narration-01 (intro)

Click a button. Grab an order. Earn a commission.

It starts with a true story from 2022.

## File: narration-02 (Part 1: The Joom trap)

Part one: the Joom trap.

In 2022, a website calling itself Joom spread quickly through Myanmar's online communities.

Facebook groups with names like "earn money at home" gathered thousands of members.

But the real Joom is a legitimate online shop based in Europe. The scammers simply copied its name.

The promise was simple: click on products to "boost their ratings", and earn a daily commission.

No real product was ever bought or sold.

To join, people handed over their phone number, an ID document, and a KPay, WavePay or crypto account.

The platform had five levels, named after famous shopping brands.

Entry cost about fifteen dollars. The top level asked for six hundred, and promised almost nine hundred dollars a month.

Over the summer, the entry deposit kept rising, from fifteen dollars, to thirty, then seventy.

To move up, members had to recruit others. They earned a cut from three levels of their team.

Recruiters showed withdrawal receipts as proof. Many of them were victims too.

There was no business behind it. The daily income was simply other people's deposits.

Early members were paid with money from late joiners. That is a pyramid scheme.

And pyramids always collapse when new money stops coming in.

One participant, living abroad without legal documents, joined in June 2022 hoping to earn from home.

The site limited withdrawals and threatened to close accounts, to keep the money inside.

When the participant realised it was a scam, they warned their team to stop depositing. But they later lost about five hundred dollars in similar schemes.

On the eighteenth of October 2022, all withdrawals stopped.

Users saw only messages like "pending", "rejected" or "technical issues".

A similar scheme called Koala, using kyat and KBZPay, collapsed at the same time. Some people had deposited more than a million kyat.

Victims estimate that Myanmar people lost many thousands of lakh kyat, more than a million US dollars.

The hardest hit were late joiners, low-income families, and migrants abroad with few other options.

## File: narration-03 (Part 2: The scam compounds)

But Joom was small compared with what was being built on Myanmar's borders.

Along the Moei River near Myawaddy, dozens of guarded compounds grew up after the 2021 coup.

The best known are Shwe Kokko and KK Park.

Many workers are lured by fake job adverts promising high salaries in Thailand.

On arrival, their passports are taken.

They are given daily targets to scam strangers online, through fake romance, investments and crypto.

Survivors describe being punished when they fail, or when they try to leave.

In 2023, the UN human rights office estimated that at least 120,000 people in Myanmar may be held in forced scam work.

The UN human rights chief, Volker Türk, said: "They are victims. They are not criminals."

In July 2026, the UN Office on Drugs and Crime estimated that scams cost victims in the Asia-Pacific region up to 114 billion dollars in 2025.

Myanmar and Cambodia are named as two of the main hubs.

## File: narration-04 (Part 3: Who protects them)

So how can compounds this large operate in the open?

Much of the land is controlled by the Karen Border Guard Force, an armed group led by Saw Chit Thu and allied with the military.

Shwe Kokko was built with a Chinese tycoon, She Zhijiang, on land controlled by the group.

In 2024 the group renamed itself the Karen National Army, but the US Treasury says it kept cooperating with the junta.

The United Kingdom sanctioned Saw Chit Thu in 2023, and the European Union followed in 2024.

In May 2025, the United States named the Karen National Army a transnational criminal organisation.

A spokesperson for the group replied that the sanctions "will not affect us in any way".

In September 2025, Washington also sanctioned people and companies behind Shwe Kokko, including She Zhijiang.

In January 2025, a Chinese actor, Wang Xing, was rescued from a scam centre in Shwe Kokko, and public anger in China grew.

In February, Thailand cut electricity, fuel and internet to border areas including Myawaddy.

In October, the junta announced a raid on KK Park, detaining more than two thousand people and seizing Starlink receivers.

By January 2026, more than six hundred buildings had reportedly been demolished.

But local people and experts called the raid a staged show, coordinated with the Border Guard Force.

No senior Border Guard Force leader has been arrested.

And in July 2026, reporters found the industry growing again, moving into homes, jungle camps and other militia areas.

## File: narration-05 (Part 4: The rigged casino)

The same networks also run illegal online gambling, aimed at people inside Myanmar.

Every slot, fish-shooting or card game is controlled by a server, not by luck.

Even an honest game returns only around ninety-five percent of bets over time. The rest is the platform's profit.

Many Myanmar sites use rigged copies of famous game brands, set to pay back far less.

Win big, and your account may be banned, blamed on a shared Wi-Fi, a "system bug", or a bot.

Or your withdrawal is "under investigation" for days, with a tiny daily limit, until you lose it back.

Some sites run fake copies of famous game servers, tuned so players almost never win.

Players report a sudden "network error" just before a jackpot, and the win disappears.

Some agents even seize the player's own deposit, knowing that illegal gamblers cannot go to the police.

Rewards come at random, the same trick psychologists use to train animals to keep pressing a lever.

Two jackpot symbols land, and the third just misses. That "near miss" is designed to make you try again.

Flashing lights and coin sounds flood the brain with dopamine, even for tiny wins.

Bet one thousand kyat, get five hundred back, and the screen still shouts "WIN!". That is a loss, disguised as a win.

Every kyat that players lose is shared out through a chain of agents.

A master agent can take up to forty percent of the losses, and pass the rest down to sub-agents.

So the agents who invite you to play earn money only when you lose.

So how do you spot a scam? First: returns that no real business could pay.

Second: "work" that is only clicking, with no real product.

Third: income that depends on recruiting people or on new deposits.

Fourth: limits, delays or excuses when you try to withdraw.

And fifth: a request for your ID together with your payment account.

Myanmar's scams are not small frauds. They are an industry, built on war, poverty and desperation.

And they survive because armed groups protect them, and those in power have looked away.
