## File: narration-01 (intro)

In February 2021, Myanmar's military seized power in a coup.

What followed became one of the biggest wars in the world today.

Yet most people outside Myanmar have never heard the full story.

In the next ten minutes, I'll explain how it started, who is fighting, and what could happen next.

Myanmar sits between India, China and Thailand, and is home to around 55 million people.

The central lowlands are home mostly to the Bamar, the largest ethnic group.

In the hills and borderlands live many ethnic peoples, like the Kachin, Karen, Shan, Chin and Rakhine.

Many of these groups have had their own armed forces for decades.

## File: narration-02 (Part 1: Seventy years of conflict)

To understand this war, we need to go back to the beginning.

Burma, as it was then called, became independent from Britain in 1948.

Fighting with ethnic armed groups began almost immediately.

In 1962, General Ne Win seized power, and the military ruled for almost fifty years.

In 1988, a mass uprising for democracy was violently crushed.

In 2011, the generals began a controlled shift to a civilian government.

And in 2015, Aung San Suu Kyi's party, the National League for Democracy, won a landslide election.

But the military kept a quarter of the seats in parliament, and control of the army, police and borders.

## File: narration-03 (Part 2: The coup)

So how did the coup happen?

In November 2020, the NLD won another landslide election.

The military claimed fraud, but election observers found no evidence of widespread cheating.

On the first of February 2021, the day the new parliament was due to meet, soldiers detained Aung San Suu Kyi and other leaders.

Army chief Min Aung Hlaing took control of the country.

The response was immediate.

Millions of people joined peaceful protests across the country.

Doctors, teachers and civil servants walked off the job, in what became known as the Civil Disobedience Movement.

Every night, people banged pots and pans, a traditional way to drive out evil.

Security forces answered with live bullets, and hundreds of protesters were killed in the first months.

## File: narration-04 (Part 3: From protest to war)

And that is when the protests turned into an armed revolution.

With peaceful protest crushed, many young people fled to the jungle and the border areas.

Elected lawmakers who escaped arrest formed a parallel government, the National Unity Government, or NUG.

In September 2021, the NUG declared a "people's defensive war".

Local resistance groups called People's Defence Forces formed across the country.

Many were trained and sheltered by ethnic armed groups that had fought the military for decades.

Today, the military faces armed opposition on almost every front.

In the far north, the Kachin Independence Army.

In the west, the Arakan Army and Chin resistance groups.

In the east, Karen and Karenni forces.

And in the central dry zone, the Bamar heartland, People's Defence Forces fight in places like Sagaing and Magway.

This matters. For the first time, many Bamar are fighting the military side by side with ethnic minorities.

## File: narration-05 (Part 4: Operation 1027)

Then, in late 2023, everything changed.

On the 27th of October 2023, three ethnic armies, known as the Three Brotherhood Alliance, launched a surprise offensive in northern Shan State.

They named it Operation 1027, after the date it began.

Within weeks, they had captured dozens of military posts and several towns along the Chinese border.

In August 2024, they took Lashio, the first regional military command headquarters ever to fall.

In Rakhine State, the Arakan Army captured the Western Command headquarters at the end of 2024.

But China, worried about its border and investments, pushed for ceasefires, and Lashio was handed back in 2025.

## File: narration-06 (Part 5: The human cost)

But behind the battle lines are ordinary people.

According to the United Nations, more than three million people have been forced from their homes.

The military relies more and more on airstrikes, which often hit villages, schools and monasteries.

Many hospitals and clinics have closed, and food prices have soared.

Short of soldiers, the military began enforcing a conscription law in February 2024.

Tens of thousands of young people fled, many of them across the border to Thailand.

Then, in March 2025, a magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck near Mandalay.

Thousands died. And even then, the fighting did not stop.

## File: narration-07 (Part 6: What happens next?)

So, where does Myanmar go from here?

The military has staged elections that critics and many governments reject as a sham, meant to give its rule a civilian face.

Resistance groups now control large parts of the borderlands, but they are divided, and the military still holds the big cities.

Neighbouring countries, especially China, have a big say in what happens next.

And through it all, millions of ordinary people still need help.

Myanmar's war rarely makes the headlines, but it affects millions of lives, and the whole region.
