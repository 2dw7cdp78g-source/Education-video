# Hunted in Exile: How Myanmar's Junta Reaches Activists in Thailand: narration script

## File: narration-01 (intro)

They kept calling, from a Myanmar number, again and again.

When he finally answered, afraid it was a family emergency, a voice said:

"Where are you now? I can guess where you are. And I know you joined the revolution."

He was not in Myanmar. He was in Thailand.

After the 2021 coup, thousands of activists, journalists, students and civil servants fled Myanmar.

They believed that crossing the border meant safety.

My research, based on interviews with seven activists living in exile, shows that for many of them, it did not.

This is the story of how a military regime hunts its critics, even after they escape.

Many of them went to Mae Sot, a Thai town just across the river from Myanmar's Karen State.

For decades, Mae Sot has been a refuge for people fleeing Myanmar's military.

Today it is home to many members of the Civil Disobedience Movement, or CDM, who refused to work for the junta.

But the military has followed them there.

## File: narration-02 (Part 1: A long arm)

There is a name for what is happening.

Researchers call it transnational repression: when a government silences its critics beyond its own borders.

It includes threats and surveillance, pressure on family members back home, online harassment, and help from the countries where exiles live.

It is usually linked to powerful states, like China, Russia or Iran.

But Myanmar's junta, a much weaker regime that called itself the State Administration Council, or SAC, does it too.

So how does a regime with limited resources still reach people abroad? That is the question my research set out to answer.

In my interviews, four tools stand out.

Direct threats.

Pressure on families.

Control through paperwork and the border system.

And surveillance online.

## File: narration-03 (Part 2: Direct threats)

Let's start with the most direct one.

One activist told me about a friend who secretly went back to Myanmar.

He was arrested at a checkpoint near Hpa-an, the capital of Karen State.

Soon after, his phone started messaging his friends in Mae Sot, asking for their home addresses.

When they asked something only he would know, the replies stopped making sense.

Then the messages changed: "We know where you are. We're going to arrest you."

Later, his mother called. Her son had been sentenced to seven years in prison.

Even routine paperwork can become a trap.

Many exiles must renew a Myanmar identity document, called a Certificate of Identity, or CI, through junta officials.

One participant described how a woman was taken to a private room and asked: "Are you a CDM?"

The officers threatened to take her document, and told her to go back to Myanmar for resignation papers.

## File: narration-04 (Part 3: Hostages at home)

But the most painful pressure doesn't target the activists themselves.

It targets their families.

Researchers call this coercion by proxy: punishing relatives at home to silence people abroad.

Several participants described family members being questioned, threatened or watched.

Strangers came asking about the houses where activists used to live.

One CDM member told me:

"After I left, they arrested my mother for forty days. Later, they arrested my uncle."

"These pressures make me afraid, even in Mae Sot."

## File: narration-05 (Part 4: The carrot)

The junta doesn't only use threats. It also makes offers.

Every participant had heard of attempts to lure exiles home, through officials, former bosses, or go-betweens.

One was promised: "If you return, we will give you your previous position and the same salary, and we will not punish you."

Sometimes, the junta shows off returnees as "forgiven".

But one CDM major who went back was never heard from again. Later, rumours said he had been sentenced to death.

## File: narration-06 (Part 5: The paper trap)

Thailand is not the one doing the targeting. But its system can make the junta's job easier.

Thailand has not signed the 1951 UN Refugee Convention, so Myanmar exiles have no legal refugee status.

To move around the country, many need a CI, a document issued by the junta itself.

As one participant put it: "Without CI, we cannot travel around Thailand."

Several participants also worried that Thai and Myanmar immigration systems share information about them.

So the very regime they fled controls the paper that lets them move.

One participant said: "They call themselves the government. But ninety percent of activists cannot get passports or CI."

Without papers, exiles risk arrest.

Participants described deportations at border gates, directly into the hands of junta officials.

Some returnees were reportedly forced into military service right after being deported.

Others described local police asking for money instead of making arrests.

The result is daily fear.

During crackdowns, people stay indoors for days.

They pay extra to have food delivered, and many fall into debt.

Some stop seeing friends at all.

## File: narration-07 (Part 6: Watched online)

And then there's the internet.

Digital repression came up again and again: hacked accounts, anonymous threats, doxxing and online monitoring.

During one online education meeting, an unknown account joined and wrote: "We know who you are. We're always watching."

The meeting was cancelled immediately.

Pro-military channels on Facebook, TikTok and Telegram spread disinformation,

stir up religious division,

drown out real news with entertainment,

and paint a false picture of a peaceful Myanmar.

Even a social media post can reach your family. Relatives get calls asking what their child is posting now.

So many people simply go quiet.

One told me: "I can't let my whole family suffer because of something I did. There are many things I want to say, but I will keep quiet about them."

## File: narration-08 (Part 7: Resisting in exile)

But this is not only a story about fear.

Activists in Mae Sot have learned to survive.

They move when they feel watched, and they are careful about who knows where they live.

CDM circles, community groups and local NGOs share legal advice, emergency help and warnings.

Many have taken digital security training to protect their accounts.

And some work quietly with lawyers and rights groups to document what is happening.

My research shows that for Myanmar's exiles, crossing the border did not end the danger.

The junta reaches them through phones, families, paperwork and the internet, and through gaps in the system of the country that hosts them.

And it works partly because it is cheap. A phone call, a visit to a parent's home, or a document renewal can be enough to make someone afraid.

Protecting them means recognising them as refugees, stopping deportations into the hands of the military, and taking online threats seriously.
